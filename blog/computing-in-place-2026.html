Computing in Place: Helping Build a Data Center Guide for Alaskans, by Alaskans

A new guide from UAF's Alaska Center for Energy and Power helps Alaska communities ask better questions about data centers. Here's what's inside, how it came together, and why I'm honored to have helped bring it to life.

Mitch Kitter Ph.D. Student, University of Alaska Fairbanks

261 miles. That's the distance from Fairbanks to Anchorage. In June, it took me 2,981 to get home, by way of Seattle. (Two canceled flights, zero rental cars, and one very creative airline agent. I told that story on LinkedIn.)

The reason I was in Fairbanks is the better story. I spent two days at the University of Alaska Fairbanks for a workshop hosted by the Alaska Center for Energy and Power (ACEP), and the guide that grew out of it is now out: Computing in Place: A Conversation Guide on Data Centers for Alaskans, by Alaskans.

A guide that starts with the community

Data centers are coming up in conversations all over Alaska, about energy, water, land, jobs, and AI. This guide doesn't argue for or against them. It helps communities ask better questions, starting with what they want for their own future rather than with any one project.

Some of the big questions it asks:

  • What computing does Alaska need?
  • Who should control it?
  • Who benefits, and who pays?

It's written for anyone helping shape the future of an Alaska community, from Tribal governments and local leaders to utilities, planners, businesses, researchers, and residents. You don't have to read it cover to cover. Jump to the section that fits the conversation you're having.

What's inside

  • A common vocabulary. "Data center" can mean anything from a gaming rig at home to a hyperscale campus. The guide breaks the term into seven types, with real Alaska examples and lessons from Iceland and Finland.
  • Right-sizing by region. The Railbelt grid and the diesel-powered microgrids off the road system face very different realities. In many isolated communities, community-scale and edge computing may be a better fit than large facilities.
  • The physical internet. A spotlight asks whether Alaska sits at an "internet cul-de-sac," with no undersea cable linking the state to Asia. The guide also makes the case that digital resilience belongs right alongside food security and energy security.
  • Community relationships. How a project could touch energy, education, public safety, subsistence, housing, water, transportation, and the local economy.
  • Working pages. Worksheets and a complete question guide you can bring straight into a community meeting, plus a "My Technology Story" exercise to get everyone talking.

How it came together

In June, ACEP brought together people from across Alaska's energy, telecommunications, computing, research, government, economic development, and community sectors, with support from the Office of Naval Research. We weren't there to judge a specific project or site. We were there to explore what kinds of data centers make sense in Alaska, under what conditions, and why.

My favorite part of this project was the collaboration. Practitioners, policy advisors, and researchers from across the state (me included) came together, shared what we know, went through a whole lot of sticky notes, and built something communities can actually use.

The conversations ran under the Chatham House Rule, so ideas could flow freely without being tied to any one person or organization. The guide isn't a transcript. It pulls the strongest ideas and questions from those two days into a resource for the whole state.

What I brought

I took part as one of the telecom voices at the table, through the lens of my Ph.D. research at the University of Alaska Fairbanks on the security and resilience of Alaska's critical telecommunications infrastructure. The perspective I brought: power gets the headlines, but connectivity decides feasibility, and resilience decides whether it lasts.

My trip home made the point better than I could. When something breaks in Alaska, the fix often takes the long way around. That's true for flights, and it's just as true for fiber, spare parts, and the people who repair them.

I also reviewed a draft of the guide, and I was glad to see the physical internet and digital resilience get the attention they deserve.

Thank you

Huge thanks to Leasi Vanessa Lee Raymond for leading this project and turning two days of conversation (and all those sticky notes) into such a great guide. Thank you to the ACEP team for convening such a thoughtful group and doing the work behind the scenes to bring it to life. Special thanks to Dr. Alec Bennett, my dissertation co-chair, for the introduction that got me in the room.

And hats off to my fellow contributors and reviewers. It was an honor to learn from, and build alongside, every one of you.

Worth every one of those 2,981 miles!
