Twenty Years of Alaska Telecom Outages in About a Minute

I worked with Claude, Anthropic's AI model, to compile 195 publicly reported outages and turn them into an animated map. Here's how we built it, what it shows, and why I'm experimenting with generative AI to make research people can see.

Mitch Kitter Ph.D. Student, University of Alaska Fairbanks

195 outages. 20 years. About one minute.

That's the Alaska Telecom Outages map, an animated look at every publicly reported telecom outage we could find across Alaska from 2006 through October 2026. Press play and watch cable breaks, storms, power failures, and equipment faults light up across the state, year by year.

How we built the list

Over one weekend in October, I worked with Claude, Anthropic's AI model, to comb through the public record: local news, provider notices, state situation reports, FCC and regulatory records, and university network-status pages. Each outage became one row in a spreadsheet, with its dates, providers, affected communities, services lost, a cause category, a short summary of what happened, and links to its sources.

The finished list has 201 outages, a handful of them going back as far as 1900. Every entry was checked against its sources (four couldn't be confirmed) and given a confidence rating. The map shows the 195 from 2006 through October 2026, backed by 328 source links from 89 outlets.

Then Claude helped me turn the spreadsheet into the map. Each outage sits at the failure site when the sources name one, or at the first community affected, and its color shows the type of cause. Alaska's subsea and terrestrial fiber is drawn for context, and the clock runs at about three seconds a year. Tap any symbol to read what happened and follow the sources.

One important caveat: this is a floor, not a census. Small-community outages are under-reported, many provider notices only live on social media, and FCC outage reports are confidential. Recent years also look busier partly because more news is online.

What it shows

  • Most outages aren't attacks. Of the 154 outages with a reported cause, only eight were deliberate: seven cases of vandalism, theft, or sabotage, and one cyber incident. The rest came from things like accidental cable cuts, storms, floods, equipment and software failures, power losses, and planned work. That says as much about what gets reported as about what happens, but resilience clearly has to cover far more than adversaries.
  • One in five has no public explanation. 41 outages were reported without any cause at all.
  • Subsea breaks are rare but long. Subsea cable damage shows up only 11 times, but it includes some of the longest outages on the map. In 2023, ice scour cut the subsea fiber serving the North Slope and Northwest Arctic, and repairs took about 14 weeks. In 2025, the same route broke again under winter sea ice. The fiber stayed down for about seven and a half months, with backups restoring partial service in the spring.
  • Alaska's connectivity depends on places far away. 13 outages started outside the state, from fiber cuts near Seattle, Tacoma, and Portland and across British Columbia and Alberta to nationwide and even global failures.
  • 911 is on the line. 40 of the 195 outages affected 911 service somewhere in Alaska.

Research people can see

My Ph.D. research at the University of Alaska Fairbanks focuses on the security and resilience of Alaska's critical telecommunications infrastructure, including how the people responsible for it perceive and prioritize risk. A table of 195 outages is accurate, but it's easy to scroll past. Watching them pile up year after year, in the same places, is a lot harder to ignore.

That's why I've been experimenting with generative AI. Claude helped me search and organize the public record, then wrote the code for the map: placing each outage, drawing the fiber, and animating twenty years into about a minute. I set the questions, made the judgment calls, and checked the work. The whole project went from a blank spreadsheet to a working map in one weekend.

And because every event is cited and the map is just a web page, anyone can explore it, check my sources, and tell me what I missed.

Where this is going

I see this as a start. Generative AI makes it possible for one researcher to build things that used to take a whole team: interactive maps, animations, and tools people can explore on their own. I'll keep experimenting with ways to make my research visible, and I'll share what works (and what doesn't) here.

A note on sources: everything on the map comes from public reports. It's part of my independent research at UAF and doesn't reflect any provider's records or views.

Watch the map

The Alaska Telecom Outages map plays on its own when it opens. You can replay it, scrub the timeline, change the speed, or filter by cause.

Know of an outage that's missing, or a detail I got wrong? I'd love to hear about it. Reach out at mrkitter@alaska.edu or connect on LinkedIn.
